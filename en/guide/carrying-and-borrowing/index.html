Carrying and borrowing without the confusion

Math practice tips · 5 min read · November 11, 2026

Carrying and borrowing are where many first and second graders get stuck. Memorising steps without understanding them leads to more mistakes as numbers get bigger. Here is a step-by-step path.

Why is it hard?

Carrying means "when the ones add up to 10 or more, move 1 to the tens". Children often don't feel why, or that the carried 1 is really ten. That leads to forgetting the carried 1, or treating a borrowed ten as one.

Step 1: Number bonds to 10

Everything rests on pairs that make 10: 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, 4 and 6, 5 and 5. Practise with fingers or counters until "7 and what make 10?" takes under a second.

Step 2: Crossing 10 with single digits

Solve sums like 8 + 5 by making 10 and adding the rest.

8 + 5 Split 5 into 2 and 3 8 + 2 = 10, 10 + 3 = 13

Step 3: Two-digit carrying

Use the same idea with bigger numbers, and ask your child to say out loud that the ten from the ones becomes 1 in the tens.

36 + 28 Ones: 6 + 8 = 14 → write 4, the ten moves to the tens Tens: 30 + 20 + 10 = 60 Answer: 64

Step 4: Borrowing

Borrowing takes a ten from the tens place. Stress that "the borrowed 1 is really 10".

52 − 27 You can't take 7 from 2, so borrow a ten 12 − 7 = 5, tens: 40 − 20 = 20 Answer: 25

Another way is to add up: from 27, add 3 to reach 30, then 22 to reach 52, so 25. Pick the method that suits your child.

Practise with the games

  • In SumSum Playground Practice, turn on addition (or subtraction) only and choose Normal (up to 20). Problems that cross 10 come up naturally.
  • Balloon Pop uses choices, so it feels easier. Move on to typing answers in Falling Problems once your child is comfortable.
  • Missed problems come back more often in the next game. Look at the review list together and ask your child to explain where it went wrong.

For parents — Instead of giving the answer straight away, ask about the process: "How many are left in the ones?" It works much better.
