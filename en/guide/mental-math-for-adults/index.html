Mental math for adults — 7 everyday calculations

Math practice tips · 5 min read · November 11, 2026

Even with a calculator in your pocket, it helps to estimate in your head first. You can tell at once whether a receipt is right or a discount is worth it. These are not school methods, only the shortcuts adults actually use.

1. Add from the left

Written addition starts from the ones, but in your head the big digits come first. If you stop halfway you already have a rough answer.

4,800 + 3,700 → 4,000 + 3,000 = 7,000 → 800 + 700 = 1,500 → 8,500

2. Round, then adjust

Three items at 9.90 is three at 10.00 minus 0.30.

9.90 × 3 → 10 × 3 = 30 → subtract 0.10 × 3 = 0.30 → 29.70

3. Sale prices: find what is left

For 30% off, do not work out 30% and subtract. Go straight to 70%: find 10% and multiply by 7.

30% off 45 → 10% is 4.50 → 4.50 × 7 = 31.50

4. Start from 10%

10% is a shift of the decimal point. 5% is half of that, 15% is the two added together, 20% is double. That covers most tips and taxes.

5. Split a bill by halving

Four people means half of a half: 68 becomes 34, then 17. For three people, look for a nearby number that divides by 3. 68 is 66 + 2, so 22 each plus about 0.70.

6. Count up for change

100 − 63.50 involves a lot of borrowing. Count up from 63.50 instead: 0.50 to reach 64, 6 to reach 70, 30 to reach 100. That is 36.50.

7. Break multiplication apart

For two-digit multiplication, split one side into tens and ones.

24 × 15 → 24 × 10 = 240 → 24 × 5 = 120 (half of 240) → 360

To multiply by 5, multiply by 10 and halve. There are more in Mental math tips.

Three minutes a day to get your speed back

Knowing a shortcut is different from having it ready. The basics, like the times tables, have to come without thinking before the shortcuts get fast.

  1. 1 minute: True or False, to read expressions quickly.
  2. 1 minute: Time Challenge with multiplication only, to bring the times tables back.
  3. 1 minute: Falling Problems, typing answers to check your accuracy.

After a week your count will be clearly higher than on day one, and because scores are saved you can see it in numbers. For keeping it going, see The five-minute habit.

Estimate first — the most useful habit is thinking "roughly how much" before the exact answer. You will also notice straight away when you mistype a digit on a calculator.
